What is a flash drought?

A drought that develops in two or three weeks instead of months, usually when a hot, dry, windy spell dries the soil fast. FlashWatch counts one when the moisture in the top 40 cm (16 inches) of soil falls from normal or wetter (the 40th percentile or above) into drought (the 20th percentile or below) and stays there for at least 10 days.

The advice

Start extra irrigation today
A flash drought is likely enough, soon enough, that starting the pivot now pays on average, and more than waiting two days for the next forecast.
Not yet: keep checking forecasts
The risk is up, but waiting for the next forecast pays more than starting now. The box says what the next forecast would have to show to change the answer.
Irrigate as usual
Flash-drought risk is low. Keep to your normal schedule; FlashWatch is not telling you to stop irrigating.
In drought now
The soil is already in drought, so there is no flash drought left to warn about. This is when irrigation matters most. Warnings start again when the soil recovers.
Frozen ground, or outside the irrigation season
No forecast while there is snow or frozen soil. Irrigation advice runs June to August; the risk numbers apply all season.

How much to irrigate

"Start extra irrigation" means run the pivot full time, so the soil is as full as it can be when the dry spell arrives. Most pivots put down less water a day than a corn crop uses in a heat wave (the farm page shows both numbers for your field), so a pivot started late can't catch up. Keep going until about an inch of rain falls or the risk drops back to normal.

Once you log what you've put on (see Your field's water), the advice says how many days of full-time pumping fill your root zone.

Your field's water

A day-by-day account of the water in your root zone, the way irrigators keep a "checkbook": rain and irrigation go in, the crop's water use comes out. Crop water use is the day's evaporation demand (from ERA5-Land temperature, humidity and sunshine, by the FAO-56 method) times a crop coefficient that follows the growth stage: low after planting, highest from about tasseling to dent. The account starts full on April 1. ERA5-Land arrives five days late, so the last five days are estimated (no rain, the past week's water use); a rain gauge reading would replace that.

Logging irrigation (by hand, or read from the pivot's own records) does two things: the account counts it, and the advice above starts from your actual deficit instead of assuming a full root zone. A field you've just filled gains less from extra irrigation, so FlashWatch can tell you when you can stop.

Flash-drought risk

Next 7 days, days 8-14: the chance that a flash drought begins at your farm in that week, from FlashWatch's forecast. It combines the state of your soil with ECMWF's weather forecasts for the coming weeks.

Normal: how often a flash drought begins in that week at this place and time of year, from 2005-2024 records. "3.7 times normal" means FlashWatch's forecast is 3.7 times that.

How good is it? On the 10% of farm-days FlashWatch rates riskiest, a flash drought begins in the next 7 days about 5 times as often as normal (see The big picture).

Soil moisture percentile

How wet the top 40 cm of soil is compared with the same time of year in 2005-2024 at this place: 50 is typical for the date, below 20 is drought. Being relative to the date matters: a low percentile in a normally wet May can still leave plenty of water, while the same percentile in late July is serious. That's one reason irrigation advice runs only in June to August, when crops use the most water. The dollar figures use your soil's actual water-holding capacity in inches, not the percentile.

The dollar figures

Start today and Decide at the next forecast are average gains per acre from extra irrigation, compared with starting the pivot the usual way (a couple of days after the crop shows stress). They come from a day-by-day water balance of your field (the FAO-56 method):

  • how much water your soil holds, and how fast your pivot can put it back;
  • how much the crop uses in a heat wave, and how much yield it loses when short of water;
  • a dry spell of 21 days, the length that matches yield losses in USDA county records;
  • your yield, price and pumping cost.

Starting early costs pumping, some of it wasted if the dry spell never comes. It pays when the yield it saves is worth more.

FlashWatch value

The season replayed one forecast at a time: every time FlashWatch said "start extra irrigation", did a flash drought follow? Each caught dry spell is worth the yield saved minus the extra pumping; each false alarm costs about a week of pumping. "Average of past seasons" repeats this for the six past seasons on file at your farm. The forecasts for each past season come from a model trained without that season.

Other actions, and rain-fed farms

For rain-fed fields (set Watering to rain-fed under Your farm), the advice box shows drought decisions instead of irrigation: cutting side-dress nitrogen in June (a crop short of water can't use it), and buying hay or feed before drought pushes prices up. These values are rougher than the irrigation model. Starting values are local where USDA publishes them (state hay prices, and how much they rose in 2012) and are labelled under each box.

Today and Replay

Today shows the latest forecast and moves on by itself each day. Replay lets you step through a past season (2006, 2009, 2012, 2015, 2018 or 2021) to see what FlashWatch would have said. The live feed isn't connected yet, so for now Today shows July 3, 2012 as a demonstration.

The map

Search for an address or town, type a latitude and longitude ("40.87, -97.99"), click the map, or drag the pin. The dashed line is FlashWatch's coverage. The drought map shows the 7-day risk compared with normal, square by square. Searches are sent to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service; nothing else about your farm leaves your browser.

Your farm's settings

Each box starts at a local value, labelled with its source: USDA prices for your state, USDA county yields, and typical figures where USDA has none. Change any of them; your values are kept in this browser and marked "Your value", with a reset link.

Morning text and pivot control

Each morning an AI agent (Meta's Muse Spark) writes a short text from FlashWatch's numbers; every figure in it is checked against them before it goes out. For farms with several pivots, an agent plans which to start and stop, checked by fixed farm rules (well capacity, water allocation, the advice). Nothing moves until you reply YES. You can also send a voice note ("corn on the sandy pivot is curling by noon"), which is transcribed and taken into account.

Data and limits

  • Soil and weather: ERA5-Land (Copernicus Climate Change Service), on a grid of about 28 km squares.
  • Weather forecasts: ECMWF's extended-range ensemble (11 runs per forecast).
  • Drought Monitor: NDMC, USDA, NOAA. Crop prices, yields and acres: USDA NASS. Place names: US Census Gazetteer.
  • FlashWatch forecasts a 28 km square, not your field. Your soil and rain can differ.
  • The dollar figures come from a textbook water balance calibrated to county yields, not from field trials.
